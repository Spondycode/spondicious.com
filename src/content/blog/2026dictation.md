## A History of Dictation

I've been keen on using dictation for a very long time. For about maybe eight or
nine years, I used Dragon Dictate before it got taken away from the Mac
platform. I liked the way that it was mostly accurate, maybe 90% to 95% accuracy
overall. I like the fact that I could, with my voice, select text and make
corrections where necessary. All I had to do was tell the computer to select a
word or a whole sentence or whatever was necessary, and then either rewrite over
the top of it or say that I wanted a word in all caps or sentence case or
whatever I needed. I could put the cursor in front of a word or behind a word
and then continue with my dictation. I was able to use Dragon Dictate for a
couple of years after they stopped being a Mac application provider. And I
looked elsewhere for something that would provide similar facilities. Dragon
Dictate wasn't cheap, and I upgraded it perhaps three or four times. For the
most part, I was happy to pay for the upgrades because it was such a useful
application, and I got work done faster by being able to dictate rather than
type.

### Apple Voice Control

Next came Apple Voice Control, which worked more or less the same as Dragon
Dictate but was slightly less accurate. The commands I needed to use to get it
to do the same sort of things for editing text were different. That involved a
bit of a learning process and a period of getting accustomed to the software.
The good thing about it is that it was available on my iOS devices just the same
as it was available on my Macs, and the customisation I put into it worked
across the whole of my system. It was disappointing that the accuracy was less
than I found with Drag and Dictate. I find it difficult to understand why he was
so poor at dealing with homonyms. On a couple of updates with the OS updates
that came on a yearly basis, I found that sometimes it would get worse. You
would think that it would understand the whole sentence and be able to
distinguish between "two" or 2 as in the number two, "too" as in something more
than, or "to" as in going to. But I kept on struggling with it and still managed
to be more productive by using voice control than using the keyboard. I still
preferred it to using Siri dictation. Because Siri dictation didn't give me any
opportunity to edit the text using voice after the text was on the page

# 2026 A Plethora Of Dictation Apps

## WisprFlow

![WisprFlow](/wisprflow1.jpeg)

Well, colour me, totally impressed. In 2025, I came across WisprFlow. There were
a few other Wispr-based dictation apps that have crossed my path, but nothing
quite as good as WisprFlow. I use the trial version of Wispr Flow, which gives
you a certain number of words per week or month. And I was considering the
possibility of paying the $12 a month for the full version of it because I
dictate a lot of words. It became a no-brainer when they came up with an offer
in November of last year, which gave me Wispr Flow for $6 a month. The accuracy
is impressive, to say the least. I don't have to say the punctuation like I did
with Dragon Dictate or with Voice Control. Those sorts of things are put in
automatically, so the commas and full stops go where they're supposed to go.

I found myself wishing that Apple could give something as good as this as part
of the operating system. When the company is making so much money and they say
that they are looking after people with accessibility problems, like not being
able to type, you would think that they would go all out to provide the best
dictation for everybody and not just for those with accessibility difficulties.
In some ways, you could categorise Apple as all fur coat and no knickers. At the
same time, AI has been around for quite a while now, and Apple have got so far
behind it is ridiculous. Siri is as dumb as a bag of spanners. We will have to
see what happens with OS27 to see if there are any upgrades to the technical
capabilities of Siri in the next Iteration of the operating system.

Wispr Flow is almost perfect. The accuracy is in the 97% to 98% range. Much of
the time, you dictate something and it comes out absolutely perfect, and you
don't need to make any changes. On the other occasion, you might need to do a
small edit. Perhaps there is a word in there that it hasn't quite heard, and you
need to fix it. Also, what's good about it is that when you do fix it, it
automatically takes that fix and adds the fixed word into the dictionary for the
app. I like that. That's impressive. Another good point for Wispr Flow is the
fact that it also has snippets. This is to take care of something where I wanted
to, for instance, put in an email, and all I have to do is to use a keyword. It
does the job for me without me having to say the whole email. Of course, that
could be something longer than an email, so again, it increases productivity
hugely by having snippets available.

Getting started with Wispr Flow was really easy, and I didn't have to do much in
the way of onboarding. I basically opened up the app and started talking to my
computer and saw text appearing on the screen. At the bottom of the screen,
there is a little pop-up which lets you know that you are actually working with
Wispr Flow. My preference with the Wispr Flow and generally with dictation
applications is to have it so that when I press the shortcut keys down to make
it work, it continues listening to me as long as I have the keys pressed. On the
other hand, some of the other applications I've tried don't give that option,
and it actually works fairly well. Also, when you press the shortcut key to
start it, do the dictation, and then press the shortcut key to stop it. Wispr
Flow also allows you to set up a key which works as a toggle, so you can have
the best of both worlds. In fact, you can even set it up to be started by
pressing one of your buttons on your mouse. I have a fancy mouse with plenty of
buttons, so it's easy for me to do that.

Wispr Flow is really, really good, and I'm happy with it. To a large extent, I'd
be okay with renewing the subscription when it comes around again in November.
However, on the subscription renewal page, it says that next year it's going to
cost me €12 a month instead of the €6 I've had it for during this last year. If
I can get it for €6 a month again in November, then I still might continue with
it rather than changing to one of the other options I have been looking at. The
other good thing about using Wisprflow is the fact that it is also available on
iOS for this one price. You don't have to pay twice to be able to have dictation
on two platforms.

## So Which Is The Dictation App For Me?

### Handy

Last week I was convinced that Handy would be the dictation app I would change
to. My first couple of sessions with it were not good, in as much as it only
offered the American version of English with those awful spellings with a Z in
rather than using the S. I like to see the word "organise" spelt properly. Same
with the word "colour". But after three or four dictation sessions, it settled
into using the British spelling, and I don't think I actually made any changes
in the settings to make that happen. It could have been that I changed the AI
being used in the background. I'm really not sure. The accuracy of Handy is
pretty good and very close to what I get with Wispr Flow. Good enough for me to
decide that when November came around, I would almost certainly change to Handy
to be my dictation application of choice. I also have a Linux machine in the
house running Linux Mint, and there is a version of Handy that works on Linux as
well, so that helped me make my decision. There isn't a version of Handy for the
iOS devices, which is a bit disappointing, and so that still weighs in my
decision whether I would stick with Wispr Flow if I can get it for the same
price as I did for the first year. I shall make my final decision on it when
November comes up and it's time for the subscription to be renewed. What I could
do to sort out that problem is to go back to using voice control on iOS and then
just try and do as much of my dictation as possible on my proper Mac computers.

![Handy Setup General](/handygeneral.png)

With using Handy it is working out well to have it with the left option left
command and space as my shortcut key. I press the two modifier keys first and
then hold down the space key and as soon as I'm finished talking I just lift up
the space key for it to stop listening to me. Within the settings, it's also
possible to change it from push to talk so that I can use those same keys to
toggle the dictation rather than holding the keys down to dictate. The settings
for the app work nicely, and it's easy to make any changes you need to get it
working the way you want it to work. You can even do things like telling it to
mute other sounds coming from the computer while you're talking.

I wasn't sure at first which transcription model I should be using, and I think
it was originally set up to use Parakeet, but after that, I changed it to
Nemotron Streaming 3.5. There were quite a few transcription models available,
and so I asked artificial intelligence to recommend the best one for me. Maybe
that's how I got it to give me British spelling rather than the American
spelling. I think that one of the advantages of Handy over Wispr Flow is the
fact that you can use it offline because the speech-to-text is working on your
own computer rather than being sent out to a server somewhere. If you are
privacy-orientated, then this is a big plus.

![Handy Models](/handymodels.png)

In the settings for handy, if you go to advanced, you can tell it to use
experimental features, which gives you another menu item called post process. I
have this set up to use a different hot key. In this case, it's Option, Shift,
and Space. I've set it up to use a local AI. I have one running on my computer
through Ollama, and I'm using gemma4:latest. It's nice to have this option
there, but I don't use it very often because it changes the text so much from
what I actually said in the same way that Eloquent from Google works. With
Handy, there is a section called "Prompt Instructions". There is a basic prompt
there, and you can add to it or change it as necessary to suit your requirements
for processing the text.

### Snaply

**Snaply Dictation -** I like this one. I set it up to use the Fn key to start
the dictation. It's also possible to change the key you use to start the
dictation and end the dictation, which is great. I can use the Fn as a toggle
key, or I can just press it once and then press it again to stop the dictation.
In the settings, it can be set so that you have both options available, or you
can do it so it's just one or the other. For myself I prefer using the holding
it down to do the dictation rather than toggle, but might as well set to do both
and then I can choose whichever feels right at the time. It also has a writing
assistant which I get to with Control + Space.

![Snaply Dictation](/snaplydictation1.png)

When you're setting the dictation language, you can choose English or you can
choose multi-language. I have mine set to English, but it will be interesting
for those people who speak more than one one language. There's a good range of
European languages to choose from.

It's easy to add languages to the custom vocabulary, and you can even do a bulk
import. So, this is for when you want to add names, acronyms, product terms, and
uncommon words, and you can add misspellings if Snaply tends to hear a term
different from what you wanted or what you expected.

![Snaply Custom vocabulary](/snaplycustom.png)

Then you have your dictation snippets, which are different from the custom
vocabulary. Basically, you can have something where you say the word email and
it puts in your actual email. Or you can have a whole load of text all thrown in
there in one go. So, this could be something like a custom prompt for use with
AI, and you'd only have to say a couple of words, and it would do the job for
you. I think the snippets feature is a huge productivity bonus using Snaply.
Snaply does a better job of both the custom vocabulary, the dictionary, and also
the dictation snippets.

You can upload a voice note. Upload any audio that you've recorded you want to
turn into text and have it transcribed, that's possible with Snaply It's not
something I tend to do, I prefer just to use dictation and put the text exactly
where I want it to go when I'm actually speaking.

**Snaply Writing Assistant -** Or there is a blue round button with a plus icon
which appears when I have text selected. This pops up a menu with the writing
assistant. There are some default options, Grammar, Polish, Custom Request,
Translate and Other Modes. Then there is a tab to chat with the writing
assistant so you can specify what you want to happen with the selected text. The
writing assistant uses an AI model of your choosing. Mine is set to a local
model - Gemma4 from Google. You can use cloud models by adding an API key. The
local model works well enough. No need to go out to the cloud, which is good for
privacy.

![Snaply Writing Assistant](/snaplywritingassistant.png)

**Meeting Notes -** another extra feature in Snaply are these meeting loads. I
haven't tried this out as I don't do meetings, and I don't know how it works out
with regards letting you know who is saying what within the meeting. You have a
few settings that you can adjust in the settings for the app, such as whether
you wanted to display a live recording indicator and whether you want it to
detect the meeting start and end automatically. You can set the maximum storage
used for meeting recordings and choose whether you want to have deeper reasoning
for meeting notes. This would increase the quality of the meeting notes, but it
would make the notes generation a bit slower. This feature of making notes for
meetings is something that Snaply has that Handy doesn't have, and is something
else which would perhaps make you err towards using Snaply rather than Handy.

## Fluid voice

This one I've only given a little bit of a try, but it's got a nice interface.
When you're setting up the application and you want to get all the settings
correct, such as choosing which model to use, this one is the same as I used
with Handy, using Nemotron Speech 3.5. According to the settings for this, it's
supposed to be ultrafast. But I don't find it particularly fast myself. It is
handy to see the text being generated in a small window at the bottom of the
screen. It's just a little bit slow. After you've finished the recording, you
have to wait a little before it puts the text into the application. That could
be because it's doing some post-processing.

![Fluid Voice](/fluidvoice1.jpeg) FluidVoice turns rough, rambling speech into
polished, ready-to-send text in any app. Free forever, open source, and 100%
on-device. So it has its good points, but on the other hand, I find it very slow
to use. It takes a long time for the text to go from the box you see at the
bottom of the screen and arrive in the app that you're dictating into. It was
disappointing right from the start, and so I didn't do much else with it.

## VoiceInk

This is an app I only had a quick look at, and the reason for that was because
after trying it out, I found Handy which is free, whereas
[VoiceInk](https://tryvoiceink.com/) you have to pay. The price is not too bad,
and it's a one off payment rather than a subscription so does that have that in
its favour. It works out at $29 for just one computer and $49 if you want to use
it on two computers. So if you have too many subscriptions and you want to save
some money, then perhaps this would be a good idea. Unless of course the
dictation you get from either Handy or Snaply is sufficient for you. At least
there is a free trial, so you can find out if it's up to your standard for
dictation and see what other features it has. It's at least worth a try if you
haven't quite found the dictation app that suits your needs yet.

# Dictation on iOS

It is kind of nice to have one application which works across platforms so that
you can have things like dictionaries and snippets working in all places where
you do your dictation. So that you don't have to remember keyboard shortcuts
that are different from platform to platform In this respect, Wispr Flow is the
winner because there is an iOS version which works pretty well, and it's also
available on the Mac.

However, I've just discovered a couple more dictation apps I can use on iOS:

- [Monologue](https://www.monologue.to/)
- [AquaVoice](https://aquavoice.com/)

Both have Mac apps too.

So far, I've only had a quick look at these two applications and they work
pretty well. On the other hand, the big downside they are rather expensive. They
do have a freemium model in-as-much as you can download them and try them out
with a limit on how many words you can actually dictate into them, and then
after that you have to pay.

**Monologue -** I downloaded and installed this application on both my Mac and
on my iPhone, and it has a very nice-looking interface. I'm going to take
advantage of the 1,000 words available of dictation in the free mode. I think
that is a one-time thousand words. It also allows you to write ten free notes.
When I started with Wisprflow, it gives you a certain number of words per month
for free. The plans and billings says that it's €149.99 per year, so that's more
than €12 month. So that makes it more expensive than the Wisprflow app.

![Monologue on iOS](/monolgue1.PNG)

It had a good onboarding process. It's on the Mac and I was quite pleased the
way it set itself up. This is using the cloud model to do the conversion from
speech to text. It's also possible to use a local model. I haven't got around to
using that yet, but I'm sure it will work fine.

IOS, same as usual. With the dictation apps, you set it up as a keyboard, and
with Monologue, you get two keyboards.

![Monologue keyboard](/monologue2.jpg)

The first keyboard has a big start button, which you press to start talking, and
then there are one or two keys underneath that you can use to add in things like
a question mark or exclamation mark, also a key, if you want to go back to
having a proper keyboard in front of you as well. One of the things with using
dictation from one of these keyboards on the iOS devices is that it will send
you off to another screen to confirm that you want to use dictation, and then
you have to swipe back, which is annoying. But that's an Apple problem rather
than a problem introduced by these apps. With Monologue, they give you the
option of creating a shortcut in the Shortcuts app, which supposedly will get
around that limitation.

On the iOS keyboard, you can change the mode from fast to specifically for
email, notes, coding, and messaging, there is another button there which gives
you modes settings, so you can make changes as you require. You can create your
own mode and give it custom settings for how you want the text to appear. I made
a change so that it always gives me British English spellings. So that it will
spell colour and organise it properly for me.

**Aqua -** this app works in a similar way and looks just as pretty as
Monologue. It's nice that it's available on both the Mac and iOS. The setup
procedure was really easy, and I was impressed with how thorough it was. In the
settings, it reckons that I can use it with the mouse as well. If I press one of
the mouse buttons, it's supposed to start listening, but I haven't got that
working yet. There could be a conflict because one of the other dictation apps
that I'm using is working with the mouse, and I need to change the settings, but
I can't find the settings for the mouse either.

![Aquavoice](/aquavoice1.jpg)

In the settings, you can add words to the dictionary for your custom words, and
there is also the one called replacements, which is the same as snippets. There
is also a box where you can insert custom instructions. So these are style
preferences or instructions. For example, use all lowercase in Slack or please
break text into paragraphs. I can't test that at the moment because this is one
that you have to pay for. Like Monologue, you get to dictate a thousand words
before you have to pay. If you like dictation as much as I do, then it's not
going to take you long to get through those 1,000 words.

![Aquavoice keyboard Settings](/aquavoice2.jpg)

There are three levels of user: Pro, Max, and Team. With the Pro version, you
get unlimited words, the Aqua engine, Avalon model, 800 custom dictionary
values, and you can tune it with custom instructions. Pro version costs $8 per
month. In the Max version, you get a real-time mode. You get voice commands like
Send It, and any new features that are supposedly coming soon, and priority
support. That one costs $24 a month. In the Team version, you get all of the Pro
features and you get team-wide settings, and it enforces privacy mode
organisation-wide. All those prices are based on paying for a whole year. So if
you just want to go month by month, then the Pro version costs $10 per month.

My initial impressions of Aqua are that it's accurate and looks good. The price
of it is reasonable when compared to WisprFlow. There is also an edit mode which
it's useful. So for example on the Mac, you could select the text and then press
your keyboard shortcut for editing, which in my case is Option and Spacebar.
Then you could say something like, "Change this word for that word."Or if it's
appropriate, you could tell it to turn the text into a list. On iOS, you get a
button in the keyboard, and you can press that and then tell it to do the
changes that you require.
